# A1 Module 1: General Engine Diagnosis

This module introduces you to how engines work and the tools and tests technicians use to figure out what is wrong when an engine is not running correctly. Think of this as learning to be an engine detective -- you will gather clues using specialized tools and tests to find the root cause of a problem.

---

## How Engines Work (4-Stroke Cycle)

Most car engines use a 4-stroke cycle to produce power. Picture a piston moving up and down inside a cylinder. Stroke 1 is **Intake**: the piston moves down and draws in a mixture of air and fuel. Stroke 2 is **Compression**: the piston moves up and squeezes that mixture tightly. Stroke 3 is **Power**: a spark plug ignites the compressed mixture, and the explosion pushes the piston down with great force. Stroke 4 is **Exhaust**: the piston moves back up and pushes the burned gases out. This cycle repeats thousands of times per minute to keep your engine running.

---

## Engine Components Overview

An engine is made up of several key parts working together. The **engine block** is the main structure that houses the cylinders. **Pistons** slide up and down inside those cylinders. **Connecting rods** link the pistons to the **crankshaft**, which converts the up-and-down motion into rotational motion that ultimately spins your wheels. On top of the block sits the **cylinder head**, which contains the **valves** that let air in and exhaust out. The **camshaft** controls when those valves open and close. Together, these parts form the mechanical heart of every vehicle.

## Diagnostic Tools (Scan Tools, Multimeters)

Modern technicians rely on electronic tools to diagnose problems. A **scan tool** (or code reader) plugs into a car's OBD-II port, usually found under the dashboard, and communicates with the vehicle's computer. It reads error codes and displays live data like engine speed and sensor readings. A **multimeter** measures electrical values such as voltage, resistance, and current. It helps you test sensors, wiring, and circuits. Together, these two tools are the foundation of modern engine diagnosis and are used daily in every repair shop.

---

## Reading Diagnostic Trouble Codes (DTCs)

When a sensor detects a problem, the engine's computer stores a **Diagnostic Trouble Code** (DTC). Each code follows a standard format. For example, P0301 means a misfire was detected in cylinder 1. The first letter indicates the system: P for powertrain, B for body, C for chassis. The numbers narrow down the specific problem. A scan tool retrieves these codes so you know where to start looking. Keep in mind that a code tells you what the computer detected, not necessarily the exact failed part -- further testing is always needed.

---

## Oil Pressure Testing

Oil pressure tells you how well the engine's lubrication system is working. Low oil pressure can lead to severe engine damage. To test it, a technician removes the oil pressure sending unit and threads in a mechanical oil pressure gauge. The engine is then started, and the gauge reading is compared to the manufacturer's specification. Normal pressure is typically 25 to 65 PSI at operating temperature, but this varies by engine. Low readings may indicate a worn oil pump, worn bearings, or simply low oil level. This is one of the first tests to perform when an oil pressure warning light comes on.

---

## Coolant Temperature Analysis

The engine's computer uses a **coolant temperature sensor** to monitor how hot the engine is running. This information affects fuel mixture, ignition timing, and emission controls. Technicians use a scan tool to read the coolant temperature in real time. If the sensor reads incorrectly, the engine may run poorly, use too much fuel, or overheat without warning. You can also use an infrared thermometer pointed at the thermostat housing to verify the sensor's accuracy. Comparing the scan tool reading to the actual temperature helps determine whether the sensor or the cooling system itself is the problem.

---

## Compression Testing Basics

A **compression test** measures how well each cylinder seals during the compression stroke. To perform it, you remove all spark plugs, disable the ignition and fuel systems, and thread a compression gauge into one cylinder at a time. Then you crank the engine and record the reading. Healthy cylinders typically produce 125 to 180 PSI, depending on the engine. All cylinders should be within about 10 percent of each other. A low reading in one cylinder could indicate worn piston rings, a leaking valve, or a blown head gasket. This is one of the most fundamental engine health tests.

---

## Cylinder Leak-Down Testing

A **cylinder leak-down test** goes a step further than a compression test by telling you exactly where air is escaping. With the piston at top dead center on the compression stroke, you pressurize the cylinder with compressed air through the spark plug hole using a special leak-down tester. You then listen and feel for escaping air. Air hissing from the tailpipe means an exhaust valve is leaking. Air coming from the intake means an intake valve is leaking. Air bubbling into the coolant indicates a head gasket failure. Air escaping from the oil filler cap points to worn piston rings. This test pinpoints the exact source of the problem.
