# A1 Module 2: Cylinder Head and Valve Train

This module covers the "top end" of the engine -- the cylinder head and all the parts that control the opening and closing of valves. Understanding these components is essential because they directly control how air and fuel enter the engine and how exhaust gases leave. Problems in this area can cause misfires, overheating, and major engine damage.

---

## What Is a Cylinder Head

The **cylinder head** is a large metal casting that bolts onto the top of the engine block. It seals the top of the cylinders and forms the combustion chamber where the air-fuel mixture is ignited. The cylinder head contains passages for coolant and oil, ports for intake and exhaust flow, and threaded holes for the spark plugs. It is typically made of aluminum for better heat dissipation, though older engines used cast iron. The bottom surface of the head must be perfectly flat to create a proper seal with the head gasket against the engine block.

---

## Valve Train Components (Camshaft, Lifters, Pushrods, Rocker Arms)

The **valve train** is the system of parts that opens and closes the engine's valves at precisely the right time. The **camshaft** is a rotating shaft with egg-shaped lobes that push against other components to open the valves. **Lifters** (also called tappets) ride on the camshaft lobes and transfer the motion upward. In pushrod engines, **pushrods** are long metal rods that carry that motion from the lifters up to the **rocker arms**, which pivot and push the valves open. Springs on each valve pull it closed again. Every part must work in harmony for the engine to run smoothly.

---

## Overhead Cam vs Pushrod Engines

There are two main engine designs based on where the camshaft is located. In a **pushrod engine** (also called OHV, overhead valve), the camshaft sits inside the engine block. It uses lifters, pushrods, and rocker arms to operate the valves in the cylinder head above. In an **overhead cam** (OHC) engine, the camshaft is mounted directly in the cylinder head, sitting right above the valves. This eliminates pushrods and allows more precise valve control at higher speeds. A **DOHC** (dual overhead cam) engine has two camshafts per head -- one for intake valves and one for exhaust valves. Most modern cars use overhead cam designs.

---

## Valve Timing and Timing Chains/Belts

**Valve timing** refers to the precise moments when intake and exhaust valves open and close relative to the piston's position. This timing is critical -- if a valve opens at the wrong time, the engine will run poorly or not at all. The camshaft is connected to the crankshaft by a **timing chain** or **timing belt** so they always rotate in sync. Timing chains are metal and typically last the life of the engine. Timing belts are made of reinforced rubber and must be replaced at manufacturer-recommended intervals, usually every 60,000 to 100,000 miles. If a timing belt breaks, it can cause catastrophic engine damage in interference engines.

---

## Valve Clearance Adjustment

**Valve clearance** (also called valve lash) is the small gap between the valve train components and the valve itself. This gap allows for metal expansion as the engine heats up. If the clearance is too tight, the valve may not close completely, leading to burned valves and compression loss. If it is too loose, the engine will be noisy and valve timing will be slightly off. Some engines use **hydraulic lifters** that automatically adjust clearance using oil pressure. Others require **manual adjustment** with feeler gauges at scheduled service intervals. Always refer to the manufacturer's specifications for the correct clearance settings.

---

## Head Gasket Function and Failure

The **head gasket** is a critical seal that sits between the engine block and the cylinder head. It must withstand extreme heat and pressure while sealing three separate systems: combustion gases in the cylinders, engine oil in the oil passages, and coolant in the water jackets. When a head gasket fails ("blows"), these systems can leak into each other. Common symptoms include white smoke from the exhaust (coolant burning in the cylinder), milky-looking oil (coolant mixing with oil), overheating, and loss of coolant with no visible leak. Head gasket failure is a serious repair that requires removing the cylinder head.

---

## Cylinder Head Warping and Resurfacing

When an engine overheats, the cylinder head can **warp** -- meaning its flat mating surface becomes slightly bent or twisted. Even a tiny amount of warping (as little as 0.002 inches) can prevent the head gasket from sealing properly, leading to leaks and repeated head gasket failures. To check for warping, a technician places a precision straightedge across the head surface and uses a feeler gauge to measure any gaps. If the head is warped beyond specification, it must be **resurfaced** (also called milling or decking) by a machine shop, which removes a thin layer of metal to restore a perfectly flat surface. If too much material must be removed, the head needs to be replaced.
