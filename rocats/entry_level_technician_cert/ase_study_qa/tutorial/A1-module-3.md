# A1 Module 3: Engine Block Assembly

This module covers the "bottom end" of the engine -- the engine block and all the heavy-duty components inside it that convert combustion force into rotational power. These parts endure tremendous stress and must be precisely measured and assembled. Understanding this module is key to performing engine rebuilds and diagnosing internal engine problems.

---

## Engine Block Structure

The **engine block** is the main structural foundation of the engine. It is a heavy casting, typically made of cast iron or aluminum, that contains the cylinders where pistons travel up and down. The block also houses the crankshaft at the bottom and provides passages for oil and coolant to flow throughout the engine. The number and arrangement of cylinders define the engine type: inline-4, V6, V8, and so on. The top surface of the block (called the deck) must be perfectly flat to seal against the head gasket. The block is designed to last the life of the vehicle, though it can crack or wear over time.

---

## Pistons and Piston Rings

**Pistons** are cylindrical metal plugs that slide up and down inside the engine's cylinders. They receive the force of combustion and transfer it downward through the connecting rod to the crankshaft. Pistons are usually made of lightweight aluminum alloy to reduce reciprocating mass. Each piston has a set of **piston rings** -- typically three. The top two rings are **compression rings** that seal combustion pressure above the piston. The bottom ring is the **oil control ring**, which scrapes excess oil off the cylinder wall and returns it to the crankcase. Worn rings cause oil burning (blue exhaust smoke) and low compression.

---

## Connecting Rods and Bearings

**Connecting rods** (or "con rods") are the links between the pistons and the crankshaft. One end attaches to the piston via a wrist pin, and the other end wraps around a crankshaft journal. The big end of the connecting rod is split into two halves held together by bolts, and **rod bearings** (thin, replaceable shell-type inserts) fit between the rod and the crankshaft. These bearings are coated with a soft metal that provides a smooth, low-friction surface lubricated by pressurized oil. Worn rod bearings produce a distinct knocking sound, especially under load, and can lead to catastrophic engine failure if not addressed.

---

## Crankshaft and Main Bearings

The **crankshaft** is a heavy, precisely machined shaft at the bottom of the engine block. Its offset journals (called "throws") convert the up-and-down motion of the pistons and connecting rods into rotational motion that ultimately drives the wheels. The crankshaft is supported by **main bearings**, which are shell-type inserts that sit in machined saddles in the block. Like rod bearings, main bearings require constant oil pressure to function properly. The crankshaft also has a **rear main seal** and **front seal** to prevent oil leaks. A worn crankshaft can be reground by a machine shop to restore proper journal dimensions.

---

## Cylinder Bore Measurement and Honing

Over time and miles of use, cylinder walls wear unevenly. **Cylinder bore measurement** uses a precision tool called a bore gauge (or dial bore gauge) to measure the cylinder's diameter at multiple points. Technicians check for **taper** (more wear at the top than the bottom) and **out-of-round** (uneven wear side to side). If wear exceeds specifications, the cylinders must be **bored** oversize and fitted with larger pistons. Even when wear is within spec, cylinders are **honed** -- a process that uses abrasive stones to create a crosshatch pattern on the cylinder wall. This crosshatch holds oil and helps new piston rings seat properly.

---

## Engine Torque Specifications

When assembling an engine, every bolt must be tightened to a specific **torque specification** measured in foot-pounds or Newton-meters. Torque specs ensure that components are clamped with the correct amount of force -- too loose and parts can leak or come apart; too tight and bolts can stretch or break, and castings can crack. Head bolts, main cap bolts, and rod bolts are especially critical. Many of these fasteners also require a **torque angle** procedure, where the bolt is first tightened to a base torque, then turned an additional specified number of degrees. Always use a calibrated torque wrench and follow the manufacturer's tightening sequence.

---

## Short Block vs Long Block

When purchasing or rebuilding an engine, you will encounter the terms **short block** and **long block**. A **short block** includes the engine block with the crankshaft, pistons, connecting rods, and bearings already installed -- essentially the complete bottom end. A **long block** includes everything in the short block plus the cylinder head(s), valve train components, and usually the oil pan and timing cover. Buying a long block means you need fewer additional parts to complete the installation. Understanding these terms helps you communicate clearly when ordering parts or discussing a rebuild with a machine shop or parts supplier.

---

## Engine Assembly Procedures

Assembling an engine requires patience, cleanliness, and attention to detail. Every part must be spotlessly clean before installation. Start by installing the **main bearings** and **crankshaft** into the block, checking clearances with Plastigage (a special wax-like strip that compresses to reveal the gap). Next, install the **pistons and connecting rods** into the cylinders using a ring compressor. Lubricate all bearing surfaces with assembly lube. Then install the **timing components**, **oil pump**, and **cylinder head** in the proper sequence. Torque all fasteners to specification and in the correct order. Finally, perform a pre-oiling procedure before the first start to ensure oil reaches all bearings immediately.
