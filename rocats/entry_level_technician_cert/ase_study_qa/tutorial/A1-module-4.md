# A1 Module 4: Lubrication and Cooling Systems

This module covers the two systems that keep your engine from destroying itself: lubrication and cooling. Oil reduces friction between moving metal parts, and the cooling system removes excess heat. If either system fails, the engine can suffer catastrophic damage in a matter of minutes. Understanding how these systems work and how to diagnose problems is essential for every technician.

---

## Oil Pump and Oil Circulation

The **oil pump** is the heart of the engine's lubrication system. Driven by the crankshaft or camshaft, it draws oil from the **oil pan** (the reservoir at the bottom of the engine) and pressurizes it. The pressurized oil is sent through the **oil filter** and then into the engine's oil galleries -- a network of drilled passages throughout the block and heads. Oil flows to the crankshaft main bearings, rod bearings, camshaft bearings, and up to the valve train components. After lubricating these parts, the oil drains back down to the oil pan by gravity, and the cycle repeats. A pressure relief valve prevents over-pressurization.

---

## Oil Types and Viscosity Ratings

Engine oil is classified by its **viscosity**, which describes how thick or thin it flows. The common rating system is set by the Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE). A typical rating like **5W-30** has two parts: the "5W" indicates the oil's flow at cold (winter) temperatures, and "30" indicates its thickness at normal operating temperature. Lower numbers mean thinner oil that flows more easily. **Conventional oil** is refined from crude petroleum. **Synthetic oil** is chemically engineered for superior performance in extreme temperatures and longer drain intervals. **Synthetic blend** mixes both types. Always use the viscosity and type recommended in the vehicle's owner's manual.

---

## Oil Filter Function

The **oil filter** removes contaminants -- tiny metal particles, dirt, and carbon -- from the engine oil before it circulates through the engine. A typical oil filter contains a pleated paper or synthetic filter media inside a metal canister. Oil enters the filter through small holes around the outside, passes through the filter media where contaminants are trapped, and exits through a center tube back into the engine. Most filters also contain a **bypass valve** that allows oil to flow even if the filter becomes clogged, because unfiltered oil is better than no oil at all. Filters should be replaced at every oil change.

## Cooling System Components (Radiator, Water Pump, Thermostat)

The cooling system removes excess heat from the engine to prevent overheating. The **water pump**, usually driven by a belt or the timing chain, circulates coolant through the engine block and cylinder head, where it absorbs heat. The hot coolant then flows to the **radiator**, a large heat exchanger at the front of the vehicle, where air flowing through its fins cools the liquid back down. The **thermostat** is a temperature-controlled valve that stays closed when the engine is cold (allowing it to warm up quickly) and opens when the coolant reaches about 195 degrees Fahrenheit, allowing coolant to flow to the radiator. The **heater core** is a small radiator inside the dashboard that provides cabin heat using the same hot coolant.

---

## Coolant Types and Mixing

**Coolant** (also called antifreeze) is a mixture of water and ethylene glycol or propylene glycol. It lowers the freezing point and raises the boiling point of the liquid in the cooling system, while also providing corrosion protection for metal parts. There are several types: **green** (IAT, Inorganic Acid Technology) is the traditional formula. **Orange** (OAT, Organic Acid Technology) and **yellow/pink** (HOAT, Hybrid OAT) are longer-lasting formulas used by different manufacturers. Never mix different types, as they can gel and clog the system. Coolant is typically mixed 50/50 with distilled water. Always use the type specified by the vehicle manufacturer.

---

## Pressure Testing Cooling System

A **cooling system pressure test** checks for leaks that may not be visible during normal operation. The technician attaches a hand-operated pressure pump to the radiator or coolant reservoir filler neck and pumps it up to the pressure rating stamped on the radiator cap (typically 13 to 18 PSI). If the pressure drops, there is a leak somewhere in the system. The technician then visually inspects hoses, connections, the radiator, water pump, and heater core for signs of seeping coolant. The **radiator cap** itself should also be tested, as a weak cap that cannot hold proper pressure will cause the system to boil over at lower temperatures.

---

## Overheating Causes and Diagnosis

Engine overheating can result from many different failures. Common causes include a **stuck-closed thermostat** (blocks coolant flow), a **failed water pump** (no circulation), **low coolant level** (often from a leak), a **clogged radiator** (restricted flow), a **blown head gasket** (combustion gases pressurizing the cooling system), or a **failed cooling fan**. To diagnose overheating, start by checking the coolant level and looking for leaks. Use an infrared thermometer to check for temperature differences across the radiator, which can reveal blockages. Feel the upper and lower radiator hoses -- if the upper hose is hot but the lower hose is cold, the thermostat may be stuck closed. A pressure test and combustion leak test can identify head gasket issues.

---

## Fan Clutch and Electric Fans

The engine cooling fan ensures enough air passes through the radiator, especially at low speeds or when the vehicle is stationary. Older rear-wheel-drive vehicles often use a **fan clutch** -- a thermostatic or fluid coupling mounted on the water pump shaft. It engages the fan when underhood temperatures rise and disengages at highway speeds (when natural airflow is sufficient) to save engine power and reduce noise. Most modern and front-wheel-drive vehicles use **electric fans** controlled by the engine computer or a coolant temperature switch. They turn on when coolant reaches a set temperature and turn off when it cools down. A failed fan or fan clutch is a common cause of overheating in stop-and-go traffic.
