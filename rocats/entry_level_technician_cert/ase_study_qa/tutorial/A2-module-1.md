# A2 Module 1: General Transmission Diagnosis

This module introduces you to the fundamentals of automatic transmissions and the diagnostic techniques technicians use to identify problems. Before you can fix a transmission, you need to understand how it works and how to figure out what is wrong.

---

## How Automatic Transmissions Work

An automatic transmission uses hydraulic fluid pressure, clutches, and gear sets to shift gears without driver input. When you put the car in "Drive," the transmission automatically selects the right gear based on vehicle speed and engine load. Inside, a complex system of valves directs fluid to engage or release clutch packs and bands, which lock different gear combinations into place. Think of it as a smart system that constantly adjusts to give you the best balance of power and fuel economy.

---

## Torque Converter Function

The torque converter sits between the engine and the transmission and acts like an automatic clutch. It is a fluid coupling -- the engine spins one side (the impeller), which pushes transmission fluid into the other side (the turbine), causing it to spin too. This allows the engine to keep running while the car is stopped. The stator, a third component inside, redirects fluid flow to multiply torque during acceleration. When cruising at highway speed, a lock-up clutch engages to create a direct mechanical connection, improving fuel efficiency.

---

## Planetary Gear Sets

Planetary gear sets are the heart of an automatic transmission's gear ratios. They consist of three parts: a sun gear in the center, planet gears that orbit around it, and a ring gear on the outside. By holding one component stationary while driving another, the transmission creates different gear ratios -- forward gears, reverse, and even neutral. Multiple planetary sets can be combined for more gear ratios. The beauty of this design is that gears are always meshed together, so shifts happen smoothly by simply redirecting hydraulic pressure to different clutches and bands.

---

## Transmission Fluid Types and Checking

Automatic transmission fluid (ATF) serves multiple roles: it lubricates moving parts, transmits hydraulic pressure, cools components, and helps engage clutches. Different vehicles require specific ATF types -- common ones include Dexron, Mercon, and manufacturer-specific formulas. To check the fluid level, most vehicles have a dipstick you pull while the engine is running and the transmission is warm. The fluid should be at the correct level and appear pink or red. Always consult the owner's manual for the correct fluid type, as using the wrong one can cause serious damage.

---

## Stall Speed Testing

A stall speed test measures the maximum RPM the engine can reach with the transmission in gear and the brakes fully applied. This tells you about the condition of the torque converter and the transmission's internal clutches. If the stall speed is too high, the clutches or bands may be slipping. If it is too low, the torque converter's one-way clutch may be faulty, or the engine may be underperforming. This test should be performed carefully and briefly -- usually no more than five seconds -- because it generates tremendous heat in the transmission fluid.

---

## Road Testing Procedures

A road test is one of the most valuable diagnostic tools. You drive the vehicle through all operating conditions -- city driving, highway merging, coasting, and stopping -- while paying attention to shift timing, shift firmness, and any unusual noises or vibrations. Note the exact speeds where shifts occur, whether the transmission slips, hunts between gears, or makes clunking sounds. A systematic road test, combined with a checklist, helps you communicate exactly what the transmission is doing so you can narrow down the problem before opening anything up.

---

## Diagnostic Trouble Codes for Transmissions

Modern automatic transmissions are electronically controlled and monitored by the vehicle's computer (PCM or TCM). When a sensor detects a problem -- such as erratic shift timing, solenoid malfunction, or incorrect fluid temperature -- the computer stores a diagnostic trouble code (DTC). Technicians use an OBD-II scan tool to read these codes. Common transmission codes start with "P07" or "P08" (for example, P0700 indicates a general transmission fault). Codes point you in the right direction but rarely tell the whole story; you still need further testing to pinpoint the exact cause.

---

## Fluid Condition Analysis

Examining the condition of the transmission fluid tells you a lot about the health of the transmission. Healthy fluid is translucent red or pink and has a slightly sweet smell. Dark brown or black fluid indicates overheating or excessive wear. A burnt smell suggests the clutch material is deteriorating. If you see metal particles or debris on the dipstick, internal components are wearing down. Some technicians also perform a "white towel test," placing a drop of fluid on a white paper towel and checking for discoloration or particles. Fluid condition is often your first clue to internal problems.
