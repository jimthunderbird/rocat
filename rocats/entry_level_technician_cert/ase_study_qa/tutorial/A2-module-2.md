# A2 Module 2: Transmission Maintenance and Adjustment

This module covers the routine maintenance and adjustment procedures that keep automatic transmissions running smoothly. Proper maintenance prevents costly repairs, and knowing how to make precise adjustments is a key skill for any transmission technician.

---

## Fluid and Filter Changes

Changing the transmission fluid and filter is the most common maintenance task. Over time, ATF breaks down from heat and collects debris from normal wear. Most manufacturers recommend a fluid and filter change every 30,000 to 60,000 miles. The process involves removing the transmission pan, draining the old fluid, replacing the filter, cleaning the pan and magnet (which catches metal particles), installing a new pan gasket, and refilling with the correct fluid type. Always check the fluid level after the change with the engine running and the transmission warm.

---

## Band Adjustment

Bands are friction devices that wrap around drums inside the transmission to hold planetary gear components stationary during certain gear ranges. Over time, bands wear and need tightening. Adjustment is done by turning a specific adjusting screw on the outside of the transmission case -- you tighten it to a specified torque (often around 72 inch-pounds) and then back it off a set number of turns as specified by the manufacturer. Not all modern transmissions have adjustable bands, but for those that do, proper adjustment is critical to avoid harsh shifts or slipping.

---

## Linkage Adjustment

The shift linkage connects the gear selector (the shifter you move in the cabin) to the manual valve inside the transmission. If the linkage is out of adjustment, the indicator on the dashboard may not match the actual gear selected, or the vehicle may not start in Park. Adjustment typically involves loosening a clamp or nut on the linkage rod, placing the transmission's manual valve in a specific position (like Neutral), aligning the selector to match, and then retightening. Proper linkage adjustment ensures reliable gear selection and correct neutral safety switch operation.

---

## Transmission Cooler Inspection

The transmission cooler keeps ATF at a safe operating temperature. Most vehicles route transmission fluid through a small heat exchanger inside the radiator, though some also use a separate air-cooled unit mounted in front of the radiator. During inspection, check the cooler lines for leaks, kinks, or corrosion. Also check for restricted flow by feeling the temperature of the lines -- both should be warm. A clogged cooler can cause overheating, which is the number one killer of automatic transmissions. If the radiator's internal cooler fails, coolant can mix with ATF, causing catastrophic damage.

---

## Line Pressure Testing

Line pressure is the main hydraulic pressure generated by the transmission's pump. Testing it tells you whether the pump, pressure regulator valve, and internal seals are working correctly. To perform the test, connect a pressure gauge to the transmission's line pressure test port, then check the reading in each gear range at idle and at a higher RPM. Compare your readings to the manufacturer's specifications. Low pressure in all ranges suggests a weak pump or worn seals. Low pressure in only one range points to a specific circuit leak. This test is essential for diagnosing slipping or harsh shifting.

---

## Governor Pressure

The governor controls shift points based on vehicle speed in older, hydraulically controlled transmissions. It produces a pressure signal that increases with vehicle speed, telling the transmission when to upshift. The governor is typically mounted on the output shaft and uses centrifugal force to regulate pressure. If the governor sticks or malfunctions, shifts may occur at the wrong speeds -- too early, too late, or not at all. In modern electronically controlled transmissions, speed sensors and the computer have replaced the mechanical governor, but understanding governor pressure is still important for working on older vehicles.

---

## Throttle Valve Adjustment

The throttle valve (TV) cable or linkage communicates engine load information to the transmission in older vehicles. It works alongside the governor to determine shift timing and shift feel. When you press the gas pedal harder, the TV signal increases pressure, causing the transmission to hold gears longer and shift more firmly. If the TV cable is too loose, shifts will be soft and early, potentially causing clutch slippage and premature wear. If it is too tight, shifts will be harsh and late. Proper adjustment requires following the manufacturer's exact procedure, which usually involves setting the cable at a specific throttle position.

---

## Shift Quality Diagnosis

Shift quality refers to how the transmission feels when it changes gears. Ideal shifts are smooth and barely noticeable. Problems include harsh or jarring shifts (too much pressure), soft or slipping shifts (too little pressure), delayed shifts, flare-ups (engine RPM jumps between gears), and hunting (repeatedly shifting back and forth between two gears). Diagnosing shift quality issues involves checking fluid level and condition, verifying line pressure, scanning for trouble codes, inspecting the throttle valve or electronic controls, and sometimes testing individual solenoids. A methodical approach helps you separate hydraulic, mechanical, and electronic causes.
