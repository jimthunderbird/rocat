# A2 Module 3: In-Vehicle and Off-Vehicle Repair

This module covers actual repair procedures performed on automatic transmissions. Some repairs can be done with the transmission still installed in the vehicle (in-vehicle), while others require removing the entire unit (off-vehicle). Knowing which is which saves time and labor.

---

## Seal Replacement

Seals prevent transmission fluid from leaking out of the case and keep hydraulic pressure contained within internal circuits. Common external seals include the front pump seal, output shaft seal, and axle seals on transaxles. When a seal wears out, you will notice fluid drips under the vehicle or low fluid levels. Replacing an external seal is often an in-vehicle repair -- you remove the leaking component, carefully extract the old seal without scratching the bore, and press in a new one. Always lubricate the new seal with ATF before installation and inspect the shaft surface for grooves that could cause the new seal to leak.

---

## Extension Housing Repair

The extension housing is the rear section of a rear-wheel-drive transmission that surrounds the output shaft. It contains a bushing that supports the output shaft and a seal that prevents fluid leaks. Common problems include a worn bushing (which causes vibration and seal failure) and a damaged housing from road debris. Repair involves removing the driveshaft, unbolting the extension housing, replacing the bushing and seal, and reinstalling everything with a new gasket. This is typically an in-vehicle repair and is a good opportunity to also inspect the output shaft for wear.

---

## Valve Body Service

The valve body is the "brain" of the hydraulic system -- a maze of channels, valves, and springs that direct fluid pressure to the correct clutch packs and bands at the right time. When valves stick or wear, you get shifting problems like delayed engagement, harsh shifts, or no shifts at all. Servicing the valve body involves removing the transmission pan, carefully unbolting the valve body, disassembling it, cleaning all components, inspecting for worn valves and damaged bores, and replacing any faulty parts. Reassembly must be precise because even a single misplaced check ball will cause problems.

---

## Solenoid Replacement

In electronically controlled transmissions, solenoids are electromagnetic valves that the computer uses to direct fluid flow. Shift solenoids control gear changes, the torque converter clutch (TCC) solenoid controls lock-up, and pressure control solenoids regulate line pressure. When a solenoid fails, you typically get a diagnostic trouble code and a noticeable shift problem. Many solenoids are accessible by removing the transmission pan and can be replaced in-vehicle. Some are individual units that bolt to the valve body, while others come as a solenoid pack. Always check the wiring connector and electrical resistance before replacing a solenoid.

---

## Servo and Accumulator Service

Servos are hydraulic pistons that apply bands, while accumulators are spring-loaded pistons that cushion the application of clutches for smoother shifts. Both are located in bores within the transmission case and can often be serviced in-vehicle by removing snap rings and extracting the pistons. Common issues include worn seals on the servo piston (causing band slippage) and broken accumulator springs (causing harsh shifts). During service, inspect the bore for scoring, replace all seals and O-rings, and check spring free-length against specifications. These are relatively simple repairs that can make a big difference in shift quality.

---

## Transmission Removal Procedures

Some repairs require removing the transmission from the vehicle entirely. This is a major procedure that involves disconnecting the battery, removing the driveshaft or axle shafts, disconnecting the shift linkage and electrical connectors, supporting the engine with a fixture, removing the crossmember or mount, unbolting the torque converter from the flexplate, and carefully lowering the transmission with a jack. Safety is critical -- transmissions are heavy (often 100 to 200 pounds or more). Always follow the manufacturer's service manual for the correct sequence, and mark or label every connector and bolt to make reinstallation easier.

---

## Clutch Pack Inspection

Clutch packs are sets of alternating friction discs and steel plates that engage to connect rotating components inside the transmission. When a clutch pack is applied by hydraulic pressure, the friction discs grip the steel plates to lock components together. During off-vehicle teardown, inspect the friction material for wear, burning, flaking, or glazing. Measure the thickness of each disc and compare it to specifications. Check the steel plates for warping, heat discoloration, and scoring. Also inspect the clutch drum for damage to the seal grooves and check ball operation. Worn clutch packs are one of the most common causes of transmission slipping.

---

## Pump Assembly Service

The transmission pump (also called the front pump or oil pump) generates all the hydraulic pressure the transmission needs to operate. It is driven directly by the torque converter and runs whenever the engine is running. The pump is located at the front of the transmission, behind the torque converter. During service, inspect the pump gears or rotors for scoring and wear, measure clearances with a feeler gauge, check the pump housing and cover for flatness, and replace the pump seal and bushing. A worn pump causes low line pressure, which leads to slipping in all gear ranges and can be confirmed with a line pressure test.

---

## Torque Converter Inspection

The torque converter should be inspected whenever the transmission is removed. Check the converter hub for scoring or grooves that would damage the pump seal. Inspect the pilot area that fits into the crankshaft. Look for cracks or damage on the converter body. Drain the fluid and check for metal debris, which indicates internal failure. Measure the converter's end play if specifications are available. If the converter was the source of contamination (visible debris in the fluid), it must be replaced -- torque converters cannot be reliably cleaned internally. A faulty converter can cause shudder during lock-up, overheating, or a whining noise at idle.
