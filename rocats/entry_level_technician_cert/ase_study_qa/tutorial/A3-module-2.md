# A3 Module 2: Transmission and Transaxle

This module covers the manual transmission and transaxle, the gearboxes that allow a driver to select different gear ratios for various driving conditions. Understanding how gears, synchronizers, and shafts work together will give you the foundation to diagnose shifting problems and perform transmission maintenance and repairs.

---

## How Manual Transmissions Work

A manual transmission is a gearbox that lets the driver choose which gear to use by moving a shift lever. Inside, there are sets of gears of different sizes mounted on shafts. By selecting a gear, you change the ratio between engine speed and wheel speed. Lower gears (like 1st and 2nd) multiply torque for starting and climbing hills, while higher gears (like 4th and 5th) allow the engine to run at lower RPMs at highway speeds for better fuel economy. The driver uses the clutch to disconnect engine power while shifting between gears.

## Gear Ratios and Synchronizers

A gear ratio describes the relationship between the input gear (from the engine) and the output gear (to the wheels). A higher ratio means more torque but less speed; a lower ratio means more speed but less torque. Synchronizers are small clutch-like devices inside the transmission that match the speeds of two gears before they engage. Without synchronizers, you would hear grinding every time you shifted. When synchronizers wear out, you may notice difficulty getting into a gear or a crunching sound during shifts, especially when the transmission is cold.

## Shift Linkage and Cables

The shift linkage is the mechanical connection between the gear shift lever inside the car and the transmission. It can be a set of metal rods, or on many front-wheel-drive cars, flexible cables. When you move the shift lever, the linkage moves selector forks inside the transmission to slide gears into position. If the linkage is worn, bent, or misadjusted, shifting can feel sloppy, vague, or difficult. Bushings in the linkage can wear out over time, introducing play and making it hard to find gears precisely.

## Transmission Fluid Types

Manual transmissions need lubrication to keep gears, bearings, and synchronizers working smoothly. The fluid type varies by manufacturer -- some use gear oil (such as 75W-90), while others specify automatic transmission fluid (ATF) or even engine oil. Always check the owner's manual or service information for the correct fluid. Using the wrong type can cause hard shifting, premature synchronizer wear, or gear damage. Transmission fluid should be checked at regular intervals and replaced according to the maintenance schedule. Look for metal particles or a burnt smell, which indicate internal wear.

## Common Transmission Problems

Grinding when shifting usually points to worn synchronizers or incorrect fluid. Popping out of gear can mean worn shift forks, detent springs, or gear teeth. Noisy operation (whining or humming) often indicates worn bearings or low fluid level. Difficulty getting into reverse is common because many transmissions do not have a synchronizer for reverse gear. Leaking fluid from seals or gaskets should be addressed promptly to prevent internal damage. A thorough diagnosis involves checking fluid condition, linkage adjustment, and sometimes listening to the transmission with a stethoscope.

## Transaxle vs Transmission

A transmission is a separate gearbox mounted behind the engine in rear-wheel-drive vehicles. A transaxle combines the transmission and the differential into a single unit, and it is used in most front-wheel-drive cars. The transaxle sits right next to the engine, and the half shafts (axles) come directly out of it to drive the front wheels. The advantage of a transaxle is a more compact layout that saves space and weight. Servicing a transaxle is similar to a transmission, but accessing some components can be more challenging due to the tighter packaging.

## Input and Output Shafts

Inside a manual transmission, the input shaft receives power from the engine through the clutch. It carries gears that mesh with gears on the output shaft (also called the mainshaft). The output shaft sends power out to the drive shaft or directly to the differential in a transaxle. These shafts spin on bearings that allow smooth, low-friction rotation. Worn shaft bearings produce noise and can eventually cause transmission failure. During a rebuild, technicians inspect both shafts for worn splines, damaged gear teeth, and bearing surface wear.

## Bearing and Seal Replacement

Bearings support the spinning shafts inside the transmission and reduce friction. Over time, bearings wear, producing whining, growling, or rumbling noises that change with vehicle speed or gear selection. Seals keep transmission fluid inside the case and prevent leaks. Common seal locations include the input shaft, output shaft, and shift rail. Replacing bearings and seals typically requires removing and disassembling the transmission. Technicians use pullers to remove old bearings and press in new ones. Seals are carefully installed to avoid damage that could cause immediate leaks.
