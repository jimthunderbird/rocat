# A3 Module 3: Drive Shaft, Axles, and Differential

This module covers the components that deliver power from the transmission all the way to the wheels. You will learn about drive shafts, axles, joints, and differentials -- the parts that let your car turn corners smoothly while keeping all the driven wheels spinning. These components vary depending on whether a vehicle is front-wheel drive, rear-wheel drive, or all-wheel drive.

---

## Drive Shaft Components

The drive shaft (also called a propeller shaft) is a long metal tube that transfers power from the transmission to the rear differential in rear-wheel-drive and four-wheel-drive vehicles. It must be precisely balanced to spin at high speeds without vibration. A typical drive shaft includes the shaft tube, universal joints at each end, and a slip yoke that allows the shaft to change length as the suspension moves up and down. If a drive shaft is dented, bent, or out of balance, you will feel vibration that increases with vehicle speed.

## Universal Joints (U-Joints)

Universal joints, or U-joints, are cross-shaped connectors that allow the drive shaft to transmit power at an angle. They are necessary because the transmission and differential are not perfectly aligned -- the suspension moves the differential up and down as you drive over bumps. A U-joint has four caps filled with needle bearings and grease. When a U-joint wears out, it creates a clunking noise when shifting between drive and reverse, or a vibration at speed. Severely worn U-joints can break, causing the drive shaft to fall and potentially dangerous loss of control.

## CV Joints and Boots

Constant velocity (CV) joints are used primarily on front-wheel-drive and many all-wheel-drive vehicles. Unlike U-joints, CV joints transmit power smoothly at sharper angles, which is essential for front wheels that must both drive and steer. Each half shaft typically has an inner CV joint (near the transmission) and an outer CV joint (near the wheel). CV joints are packed with grease and sealed inside rubber boots. When a boot tears, grease leaks out and dirt gets in, quickly destroying the joint. A clicking noise during turns is a classic sign of a worn outer CV joint.

## Half Shafts

Half shafts (also called CV axles or drive axles) are the short shafts that connect the transaxle or differential to the wheels on independently suspended vehicles. Each half shaft has a CV joint on each end. On front-wheel-drive cars, the half shafts come out of the transaxle and go to each front wheel. They must accommodate the up-and-down motion of the suspension and the steering angle of the front wheels. Half shafts are usually replaced as complete assemblies when a CV joint fails, since rebuilt units are affordable and reliable.

## Differential Operation

The differential is a gearbox that splits engine power between two wheels while allowing them to spin at different speeds. This is necessary because when a car turns, the outer wheel must travel a greater distance than the inner wheel and therefore must spin faster. Inside the differential, a set of gears -- called spider gears or side gears -- allows this speed difference. Without a differential, the tires would scrub and skip during turns. The differential is housed in its own case and filled with gear oil that must be changed at regular intervals.

## Ring and Pinion Gears

The ring and pinion gears are the main gear set inside the differential. The pinion gear is connected to the drive shaft and meshes with the larger ring gear, which turns the axle shafts. This gear set changes the direction of power flow by 90 degrees (from the lengthwise drive shaft to the crosswise axles) and provides a final gear reduction. The ratio of ring gear teeth to pinion gear teeth determines the final drive ratio. A higher numerical ratio (like 4.10:1) provides more acceleration, while a lower ratio (like 3.08:1) is better for highway fuel economy.

## Limited-Slip Differential

A standard open differential sends power to the wheel with the least traction, which is a problem if one wheel is on ice or mud. A limited-slip differential (LSD) overcomes this by using clutch packs, gears, or a viscous fluid to transfer some torque to the wheel that still has grip. This improves traction in slippery conditions and during hard acceleration. Limited-slip differentials require special friction-modifier additives in their gear oil. Without the correct fluid, an LSD can chatter or make a clunking noise during tight turns, such as in parking lots.

## Four-Wheel Drive and All-Wheel Drive Systems

Four-wheel drive (4WD) systems are typically part-time, meaning the driver selects when to engage power to all four wheels using a lever or switch. These are common on trucks and SUVs. All-wheel drive (AWD) systems work automatically and send power to all four wheels full-time, adjusting the split based on traction. Both systems use additional components like a transfer case or center differential to distribute power between the front and rear axles. Understanding the differences helps technicians choose the right diagnostic and repair approach for each system.

## Transfer Case Function

The transfer case is a gearbox found in four-wheel-drive and many all-wheel-drive vehicles. It sits between the transmission and the front and rear drive shafts, splitting engine power between the two axles. Many transfer cases include a low-range gear set that multiplies torque for off-road driving or heavy towing. Transfer cases use their own lubricant (usually ATF or gear oil) and have chains or gear sets inside. Common problems include fluid leaks, worn chains, and difficulty shifting between 2WD and 4WD modes. Regular fluid changes help extend transfer case life.
