# A4 Module 3: Wheel Alignment and Tires

## What Is Wheel Alignment

Wheel alignment is the process of adjusting the angles of the wheels so they are set to the vehicle manufacturer's specifications. Proper alignment ensures the tires make correct contact with the road, the vehicle drives straight, and the tires wear evenly. Alignment is not about adjusting the tires themselves -- it is about adjusting the suspension components that determine wheel angles. Hitting a pothole, curb, or worn suspension parts can throw alignment off. Technicians use a computerized alignment machine to measure and correct these angles.

## Camber Angle

Camber is the inward or outward tilt of the tire when viewed from the front of the vehicle. If the top of the tire leans outward, that is positive camber. If it leans inward, that is negative camber. Zero camber means the tire is perfectly vertical. Incorrect camber causes the tire to wear heavily on one edge -- the inner edge for negative camber and the outer edge for positive camber. Camber is adjusted by moving the top or bottom of the steering knuckle. Worn ball joints and control arm bushings often cause camber to shift.

## Caster Angle

Caster is the angle of the steering axis when viewed from the side of the vehicle. Imagine a line drawn through the upper and lower steering pivots. If this line tilts toward the rear of the vehicle at the top, that is positive caster. Positive caster helps the steering wheel return to center after a turn and provides straight-line stability at highway speeds. Most modern vehicles have positive caster. Unequal caster side to side causes the vehicle to pull toward the side with less caster. Caster usually is not adjustable on all vehicles.

## Toe Angle

Toe describes whether the front edges of the tires point inward or outward when viewed from above. If the fronts of the tires angle toward each other, that is toe-in. If they angle away from each other, that is toe-out. Toe is the most commonly adjusted alignment angle and has the greatest effect on tire wear. Incorrect toe causes a feathered or scalloped wear pattern across the tread. Technicians adjust toe by turning the tie rod adjustment sleeves. Even small toe misalignment can rapidly destroy a set of tires.

## Thrust Angle

The thrust angle is the direction the rear wheels are pointing compared to the vehicle's centerline. Ideally the rear wheels aim straight ahead, giving a thrust angle of zero. If the rear wheels point slightly to one side, the vehicle will try to travel in that direction, and the driver must hold the steering wheel off-center to drive straight. This is sometimes called dog-tracking. A four-wheel alignment checks and corrects the thrust angle. On vehicles with a non-adjustable rear, the front alignment is set relative to the thrust angle.

## Tire Types and Sizes

Tires come in several types for different conditions: all-season tires are the most common, summer or performance tires offer better grip in warm weather, and winter tires provide traction in snow and ice. Tire size is printed on the sidewall in a format like P215/65R15. The P means passenger, 215 is the width in millimeters, 65 is the aspect ratio (sidewall height as a percentage of width), R means radial construction, and 15 is the wheel diameter in inches. Always replace tires with the correct size specified for your vehicle.

## Tire Pressure Monitoring System (TPMS)

TPMS is a safety system required on all vehicles sold in the United States since 2008. It alerts the driver when a tire's pressure drops significantly below the recommended level. There are two types: direct TPMS uses a pressure sensor inside each tire that sends a wireless signal to the vehicle's computer, and indirect TPMS uses the ABS wheel speed sensors to detect a low tire by its slightly different rolling speed. When servicing tires, technicians must handle TPMS sensors carefully and may need to reprogram them after tire rotation.

## Tire Rotation Patterns

Tire rotation means moving tires to different positions on the vehicle to promote even wear. Front tires typically wear differently than rears because they handle steering forces. Common rotation patterns include front-to-rear (for directional tires), cross pattern (front tires cross to the opposite rear, rears move straight forward), and modified X-pattern. Most manufacturers recommend rotation every 5,000 to 8,000 miles. Regular rotation extends the life of a full set of tires and helps maintain balanced handling. Always check the owner's manual for the recommended pattern.

## Wheel Balancing

Wheel balancing ensures the weight of the tire and wheel assembly is distributed evenly around the axle. Even small imbalances cause the wheel to vibrate at certain speeds, typically felt as a shimmy in the steering wheel (front) or the seat (rear). A technician mounts the assembly on a balancing machine that spins it and identifies heavy spots. Small clip-on or adhesive weights are then placed at precise locations to counterbalance. Balancing should be done whenever a tire is mounted, replaced, or when vibration is felt during driving.

## Tire Wear Patterns and Diagnosis

Reading tire wear patterns tells a technician a lot about the vehicle's condition. Wear on both edges suggests under-inflation. Wear down the center indicates over-inflation. Wear on only one edge points to a camber problem. Feathered wear across the tread is a toe alignment issue. Cupping or scalloping in patches usually means worn shocks or struts. Flat spots can result from hard braking or a locked wheel. Always inspect wear patterns before an alignment to identify underlying suspension or inflation problems. Replace tires that reach the minimum tread depth of 2/32 of an inch.
