# A5 Module 1: Hydraulic System and General Diagnosis

This module introduces the hydraulic brake system -- the foundation of how modern vehicles stop. You will learn how fluid pressure translates your foot on the pedal into stopping force at the wheels, and how to diagnose problems when something goes wrong.

---

## How Hydraulic Brakes Work

Hydraulic brakes use a liquid (brake fluid) sealed inside tubes and cylinders to transmit force. When you press the brake pedal, you push a piston inside the master cylinder. This creates pressure in the brake fluid, which travels through brake lines to each wheel. At each wheel, the pressure pushes pistons that squeeze brake pads against a rotor (disc brakes) or push shoes against a drum. Because liquids cannot be compressed, the force transfers almost instantly. Think of it like squeezing one end of a sealed water balloon -- the other end pushes outward. This principle, called Pascal's Law, is what makes hydraulic brakes reliable and powerful.

## Brake Fluid Types (DOT 3, 4, 5, 5.1)

Brake fluid is the lifeblood of the hydraulic system. It must handle extreme heat without boiling, because boiling fluid creates gas bubbles that compress and cause brake failure. There are several types classified by the Department of Transportation (DOT). DOT 3 is the most common and works for everyday driving. DOT 4 has a higher boiling point and is used in vehicles that generate more heat. DOT 5 is silicone-based, does not absorb water, and is used in specialty applications -- it should never be mixed with other types. DOT 5.1 is glycol-based like DOT 3 and 4, but has a boiling point similar to DOT 5. Always use the fluid type specified by the vehicle manufacturer.

## Master Cylinder Function

The master cylinder is the heart of the hydraulic brake system. It sits on the firewall (the wall between the engine and the cabin) and connects directly to the brake pedal. When you press the pedal, a pushrod moves pistons inside the master cylinder, pressurizing the brake fluid. Modern master cylinders are "dual" or "tandem" designs, meaning they have two separate chambers. Each chamber supplies fluid to a different set of wheels (usually front-left/rear-right and front-right/rear-left). This way, if one circuit leaks, the other still works and you can stop the car. On top of the master cylinder sits a reservoir that holds extra brake fluid.

## Brake Lines and Hoses

Brake lines carry pressurized fluid from the master cylinder to each wheel. The main lines running along the vehicle frame are made of double-walled steel tubing for strength and corrosion resistance. Near each wheel, steel lines connect to flexible rubber hoses. These hoses allow the wheels and suspension to move up and down without breaking the connection. Over time, rubber hoses can swell, crack, or deteriorate internally, which can restrict fluid flow or cause leaks. Steel lines can rust, especially in areas where road salt is used. Technicians must inspect both lines and hoses regularly for damage, leaks, and wear.

## Proportioning Valve

When you brake hard, the vehicle's weight shifts forward. This means the front wheels carry more load and need more braking force, while the rear wheels carry less and can lock up easily. The proportioning valve solves this problem by reducing hydraulic pressure to the rear brakes during hard stops. This prevents the rear wheels from locking up and causing a skid. Some vehicles use a height-sensing proportioning valve that adjusts based on how much weight is in the rear of the vehicle, such as in a pickup truck. Many newer vehicles replace the proportioning valve with electronic control through the ABS system.

## Brake Bleeding Procedures

Air trapped in the brake lines is a serious problem. Unlike liquid, air compresses, so air bubbles make the pedal feel spongy and reduce stopping power. Brake bleeding is the process of removing air from the hydraulic system. The most common method is manual bleeding: one person pumps the brake pedal while another opens a bleeder valve at each wheel to let air and old fluid escape. Other methods include gravity bleeding (letting fluid flow out on its own), vacuum bleeding (using a pump to draw fluid through), and pressure bleeding (pushing fluid through with a pressurized tool). Always bleed in the correct order, typically starting at the wheel farthest from the master cylinder.

## Brake Pedal Diagnosis (Spongy, Hard, Low)

The way the brake pedal feels tells a technician a lot about the system's health. A **spongy or soft pedal** usually means there is air in the hydraulic lines -- bleeding the system is the fix. A **hard pedal** that requires excessive force suggests a problem with the power brake booster or its vacuum supply. A **low pedal** that sinks close to the floor may indicate worn brake pads, a fluid leak, or a failing master cylinder. A pedal that **slowly sinks to the floor** while you hold steady pressure is a classic sign of an internal master cylinder leak. Learning to read pedal feel is one of the most valuable diagnostic skills for a brake technician.

## ABS Warning Light Diagnosis

The ABS (Anti-lock Braking System) warning light on the dashboard tells you the electronic brake control system has detected a problem. When this light comes on, the regular brakes still work, but the anti-lock function may be disabled. Common causes include a faulty wheel speed sensor, damaged wiring, a bad ABS module, or low brake fluid. Technicians use a scan tool to read diagnostic trouble codes (DTCs) stored in the ABS control module. These codes point to the specific component or circuit that triggered the light. Always address ABS warnings promptly, because the system is there to prevent wheel lockup during emergency stops.
