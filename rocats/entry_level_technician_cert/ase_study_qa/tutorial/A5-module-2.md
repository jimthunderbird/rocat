# A5 Module 2: Disc and Drum Brakes

This module covers the two types of friction brakes you will encounter on vehicles: disc brakes and drum brakes. Most modern cars use disc brakes on the front wheels (and often all four), while drum brakes are still found on the rear of many economy vehicles. Understanding both designs and their service procedures is essential for the A5 exam.

---

## How Disc Brakes Work

Disc brakes work like squeezing a spinning disc between your fingers to slow it down. A metal rotor (the disc) is bolted to the wheel hub, so it spins with the wheel. A caliper straddles the rotor and holds the brake pads. When you press the brake pedal, hydraulic pressure pushes pistons inside the caliper, which press the brake pads against both sides of the spinning rotor. The friction between the pads and the rotor converts the vehicle's motion (kinetic energy) into heat, slowing the car. Disc brakes are popular because they handle heat well, resist fade during repeated hard stops, and dry out quickly in wet conditions.

## Brake Pads and Rotors

Brake pads are the replaceable friction material that presses against the rotor. They come in several material types: organic (quiet but wears faster), semi-metallic (durable and good heat handling, but noisier), and ceramic (quiet, low dust, and long-lasting). Pads include a metal backing plate and a wear indicator -- a small metal tab that squeals when the pad is nearly worn out. Rotors are usually made of cast iron or a composite material. Some rotors are solid, while higher-performance ones are ventilated with internal vanes for better cooling. Rotors must be smooth and within the manufacturer's minimum thickness specification to work properly.

## Brake Caliper Types (Floating vs Fixed)

There are two main caliper designs. A **floating caliper** (also called a sliding caliper) has a piston on only one side. When hydraulic pressure pushes the piston, it presses the inner pad against the rotor, and then the caliper body slides on pins to pull the outer pad against the other side. Floating calipers are simpler, lighter, and less expensive, so they are the most common type. A **fixed caliper** is bolted rigidly in place and has pistons on both sides of the rotor. Fixed calipers provide more even clamping force and better performance, but they cost more and are mainly found on performance and luxury vehicles.

## Rotor Measurement and Resurfacing

Rotors wear down over time and must be inspected during brake service. Technicians measure two key things: **thickness** and **runout**. Thickness is checked with a micrometer to ensure the rotor has not worn below the manufacturer's minimum specification (stamped on the rotor or listed in a service manual). Lateral runout -- a wobble in the rotor -- is measured with a dial indicator and can cause pedal pulsation. If a rotor is too thin, it must be replaced. If it has minor surface imperfections but is still thick enough, it can be resurfaced (machined) on a brake lathe to create a smooth, flat surface for the new pads. Always check thickness after resurfacing.

## How Drum Brakes Work

Drum brakes use a hollow, bowl-shaped drum that is bolted to the wheel hub and spins with the wheel. Inside the drum, two curved brake shoes sit on a backing plate. When you press the brake pedal, hydraulic pressure pushes the pistons inside the wheel cylinder outward, forcing the brake shoes against the inner surface of the spinning drum. The friction between the shoes and drum slows the vehicle. Drum brakes are less expensive than disc brakes and provide good parking brake function, which is why they are still used on the rear wheels of many vehicles.

## Brake Shoes and Drums

Brake shoes are curved metal pieces with friction material (lining) bonded or riveted to one side. Most drum brake setups use two shoes per wheel: a primary (leading) shoe and a secondary (trailing) shoe. The secondary shoe is usually longer because it does more of the braking work due to a self-energizing effect -- the drum's rotation helps push the shoe harder against the surface. Drums are typically made of cast iron. Over time, the inside of the drum wears and develops a ridge at the edge. Drums are measured with a drum gauge to check for maximum diameter; if they are too large (worn too much), they must be replaced.

## Wheel Cylinder Function

The wheel cylinder is the hydraulic component inside a drum brake assembly. It is a small cylinder mounted on the backing plate at the top, between the two brake shoes. It contains two pistons that face outward in opposite directions, with seals (cups) to prevent fluid leaks. When brake fluid pressure enters the cylinder, it pushes both pistons outward, which pushes the tops of the brake shoes apart and into contact with the drum. Wheel cylinders can develop leaks over time as the internal seals wear out. A leaking wheel cylinder will cause brake fluid to appear inside the drum and may lead to reduced braking and a low pedal.

## Drum Brake Adjustment

As drum brake shoes wear down, the gap between the shoes and the drum increases. If this gap gets too large, you have to push the pedal farther before the shoes contact the drum, resulting in a low pedal. Drum brakes must be adjusted to maintain the correct clearance. Manual adjustment is done using a star wheel adjuster -- a threaded device between the bottom of the two shoes. Turning the star wheel with a brake spoon (a special tool inserted through a slot in the backing plate or drum) expands or contracts the shoes. The goal is to set the shoes as close to the drum as possible without dragging.

## Self-Adjuster Mechanism

Most modern drum brakes include an automatic self-adjuster so the driver does not have to worry about manual adjustments. The self-adjuster uses a cable or lever connected to the star wheel adjuster. Each time the driver applies the brakes while backing up (or in some designs, during forward stops), the mechanism checks whether the shoe travel is too large. If it is, the mechanism clicks the star wheel one notch, slightly expanding the shoes to take up the extra gap. Over time, this keeps the shoe-to-drum clearance consistent. During brake service, technicians should verify that the self-adjuster is clean, properly assembled, and moving freely.
