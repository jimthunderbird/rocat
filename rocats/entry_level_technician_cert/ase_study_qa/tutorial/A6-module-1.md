# A6 Module 1: General Electrical Diagnosis and Battery

This module introduces the building blocks of automotive electricity and the battery that powers it all. If you are new to car mechanics, start here -- everything else in the electrical system depends on these fundamentals.

---

## Basic Electricity (Voltage, Current, Resistance)

Electricity in a car works a lot like water flowing through a hose. **Voltage** is the pressure pushing the electricity forward, measured in volts (V). **Current** is the amount of electricity flowing, measured in amps (A). **Resistance** is anything that slows the flow down, measured in ohms. A healthy car battery produces about 12.6 volts. Every wire, switch, and component in the vehicle either uses, controls, or resists the flow of electrical current. Understanding these three properties is the foundation for diagnosing any electrical problem.

## Ohm's Law

Ohm's Law is the single most important formula in electrical work. It states that **Voltage = Current x Resistance**, or V = I x R. If you know any two of the three values, you can calculate the third. For example, if a headlight bulb draws 5 amps on a 12-volt system, its resistance is 2.4 ohms (12 / 5 = 2.4). Technicians use Ohm's Law constantly to predict what readings they should see when testing circuits, helping them pinpoint whether a problem is caused by too much resistance, too little voltage, or an incorrect current draw.

## Using a Multimeter

A multimeter is your most essential diagnostic tool. It measures voltage, current, and resistance. To measure voltage, set the dial to "V DC" and touch the red probe to the positive side of the circuit and the black probe to ground. To measure resistance, disconnect the component first and set the dial to ohms. Most automotive multimeters also have a continuity setting that beeps when a wire has a complete path. Always start with a known-good battery and practice on simple circuits before diagnosing complex problems.

## Wiring Diagrams and Schematics

Wiring diagrams are maps of a vehicle's electrical system. They use standardized symbols to represent components like switches, motors, grounds, and connectors. Lines represent wires, and each wire is labeled with a color code and circuit number. At first, these diagrams can look overwhelming, but you read them the same way every time: find the power source at the top, follow the path through switches and loads, and trace it to ground at the bottom. Service manuals and online databases provide vehicle-specific diagrams that are essential for tracing faults.

## Fuses and Relays

Fuses protect wires and components from damage caused by too much current. Each fuse is rated for a specific amperage -- if the current exceeds that rating, a thin metal strip inside the fuse melts and breaks the circuit. You can visually inspect a fuse or test it with a multimeter. Relays are electrically controlled switches that allow a small current from a switch to activate a larger current for things like headlights or the fuel pump. A relay clicks when energized. Both are usually located in the fuse box under the hood or dashboard.

## Battery Construction and Types

A standard car battery is a 12-volt lead-acid battery made up of six cells, each producing about 2.1 volts. Inside each cell, lead plates sit in a sulfuric acid solution called electrolyte. When the battery discharges, a chemical reaction between the lead and acid produces electricity. The most common types are **flooded** (traditional, with liquid electrolyte), **AGM** (Absorbed Glass Mat, where the electrolyte is held in fiberglass mats), and **gel** batteries. AGM batteries are becoming more popular because they handle deep discharges better and are maintenance-free.

## Battery Testing (Load Test, Conductance Test)

Two main tests tell you if a battery is healthy. A **load test** applies a heavy electrical load (typically half the battery's cold cranking amps) for 15 seconds while monitoring voltage. A good battery stays above 9.6 volts during the test. A **conductance test** sends a small AC signal through the battery and measures how well the plates conduct electricity. Conductance testers are faster, safer, and can test batteries that are partially discharged. Always check the battery's state of charge before testing -- a discharged battery may fail a test even if it is actually still good.

## Battery Charging

When a battery is low, you can recharge it with an external charger. A slow charge at 2 to 10 amps is gentler on the battery and produces a more complete charge than a fast charge. Most modern chargers are "smart" chargers that automatically adjust the charging rate and shut off when the battery is full. Never charge a frozen battery -- let it thaw first. Always charge in a well-ventilated area because lead-acid batteries produce hydrogen gas during charging, which is flammable. Check the electrolyte level in flooded batteries before and after charging.

## Jump Starting Procedures

Jump starting uses a good battery to start a vehicle with a dead battery. Connect the **red** (positive) cable to the positive terminal of the dead battery first, then to the positive terminal of the good battery. Connect the **black** (negative) cable to the negative terminal of the good battery, then to an unpainted metal surface on the dead vehicle's engine block -- not the dead battery's negative terminal. This avoids sparks near the battery. Start the good vehicle, wait a minute, then start the dead vehicle. Remove cables in reverse order. Incorrect connections can damage electronics or cause injury.
