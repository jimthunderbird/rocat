# A6 Module 3: Lighting, Gauges, and Accessories

This module covers the electrical systems you interact with every day as a driver -- lights, dashboard gauges, and convenience features like power windows. These systems make the vehicle safe, comfortable, and road-legal. For a technician, diagnosing problems in these circuits requires solid knowledge of wiring diagrams, switches, relays, and the body control module that manages many of them.

---

## Headlight Systems (Halogen, HID, LED)

Modern vehicles use one of three headlight technologies. **Halogen** bulbs are the most common and affordable. They use a tungsten filament inside a gas-filled glass envelope and typically last 500 to 1,000 hours. **HID** (High-Intensity Discharge) headlights produce light by creating an electrical arc between two electrodes inside a gas-filled capsule. They are brighter than halogen but require a ballast module to generate the high startup voltage. **LED** headlights use light-emitting diodes and are the most energy-efficient and longest-lasting. Each type has different replacement procedures and circuit requirements.

## Turn Signals and Hazard Lights

Turn signals use a flasher module to blink the front and rear indicator lights on one side of the vehicle. In older cars, a thermal flasher heated a bimetallic strip to open and close the circuit. Modern vehicles use an electronic flasher controlled by the body control module. If a bulb burns out, the flash rate often increases (hyperflash) to alert the driver. Hazard lights activate all four turn signals simultaneously and use the same bulbs but typically a separate switch and circuit. Always check for proper flash rate, bulb brightness, and correct left/right operation during diagnosis.

## Brake Lights and Tail Lights

Tail lights come on with the headlights and make the vehicle visible from behind. Brake lights are brighter and activate when the driver presses the brake pedal, controlled by a brake light switch mounted near the pedal. Many vehicles use a dual-filament bulb where one filament is for the tail light and the brighter filament is for the brake light. If brake lights stay on constantly, the brake light switch may be out of adjustment. If they do not work at all, check the switch, fuse, and bulb. Third brake lights (center high-mounted stop lights) are required on all modern vehicles.

## Instrument Cluster Gauges

The instrument cluster displays critical vehicle information to the driver. Common gauges include the speedometer, tachometer (RPM), fuel level, and engine temperature. Older vehicles used mechanical cables and analog sending units. Modern vehicles use electronic sensors that send data to the instrument cluster via wiring or a data network (CAN bus). The fuel gauge, for example, reads a variable resistor attached to a float in the fuel tank. When a gauge reads incorrectly, the problem could be the sensor, the wiring, or the gauge itself. A scan tool can often command a gauge self-test to verify cluster function.

## Warning Lights and Indicators

Warning lights on the dashboard alert the driver to problems. The check engine light (MIL) indicates an emissions-related fault. The oil pressure light warns of dangerously low oil pressure. The battery or charging system light means the alternator is not keeping up. The ABS light signals an issue with the anti-lock braking system. Most warning lights perform a bulb check when you first turn the key on -- they should all illuminate briefly, then turn off. If a warning light stays on, use a scan tool to read diagnostic trouble codes (DTCs) and identify the specific issue.

## Horn Circuit

The horn circuit is one of the simplest in the vehicle. When you press the horn button on the steering wheel, it completes a ground path through the clock spring (a coiled ribbon cable that maintains electrical connections as the steering wheel turns). This energizes a relay in the fuse box, which sends battery voltage to the horn. The horn itself is an electromagnetic device with a vibrating diaphragm that produces sound. If the horn does not work, check the fuse first, then the relay, then the horn button and clock spring. You can test the horn directly by applying 12 volts to it.

## Windshield Wiper and Washer System

The wiper system uses an electric motor to move the wiper arms across the windshield. A wiper switch provides multiple speed settings by changing the voltage or ground path to the motor. Intermittent (delay) wipers use a timer circuit or body control module to pulse the motor at intervals. The wiper motor has a "park" switch inside that keeps the motor running until the blades reach their rest position, even after you turn them off. The washer system uses a small electric pump to spray fluid from a reservoir through nozzles onto the windshield. Common failures include worn wiper linkage, a dead pump, and clogged nozzles.

## Power Windows and Door Locks

Power windows use small reversible electric motors inside each door to raise and lower the glass. The driver's door has a master switch that can control all windows and often includes a lockout button to disable rear passenger switches. The motor drives a regulator mechanism (cable or scissor type) that moves the glass. Power door locks use small actuators in each door, controlled by the key fob, door switches, or the body control module. If one window or lock fails, the problem is usually the individual motor or switch. If all fail simultaneously, check the fuse or the master control module.

## Body Control Module (BCM)

The body control module is a computer that manages many of the vehicle's electrical accessories. It controls or coordinates interior and exterior lighting, power windows, door locks, the horn, wipers, the security system, and more. The BCM communicates with other modules through the vehicle's data network (CAN bus). It receives inputs from switches, sensors, and key fobs, then activates the appropriate outputs. When diagnosing accessory issues on modern vehicles, always check for BCM trouble codes with a scan tool. A faulty BCM can cause multiple seemingly unrelated electrical problems across the vehicle.
