# A7 Module 1: A/C System Fundamentals

This module introduces you to how automotive air conditioning works. Think of an A/C system as a heat mover -- it does not create cold air, it removes heat from the cabin and dumps it outside the vehicle. Understanding the refrigeration cycle and each component's role is the foundation for all A/C diagnosis and repair work.

---

## How Automotive A/C Works

Your car's A/C system works by circulating a special fluid called refrigerant through a closed loop of components. The refrigerant absorbs heat from the air inside the cabin, carries that heat outside, and releases it into the outside air. This process repeats continuously while the A/C is running. The key principle is that refrigerant changes between liquid and gas states, and these phase changes allow it to absorb and release large amounts of heat very efficiently.

---

## Refrigeration Cycle Basics

The refrigeration cycle has four main stages. First, the compressor pressurizes the refrigerant gas, making it hot. Second, the hot gas flows to the condenser where it cools down and turns into a high-pressure liquid. Third, the liquid passes through an expansion device that drops its pressure, making it very cold. Fourth, the cold refrigerant flows through the evaporator where it absorbs heat from the cabin air and turns back into a gas. The gas returns to the compressor and the cycle starts again.

---

## Refrigerant Types (R-134a, R-1234yf)

Refrigerant is the fluid that carries heat through the A/C system. Most vehicles built from the mid-1990s through the mid-2010s use **R-134a**. Newer vehicles are switching to **R-1234yf**, which is more environmentally friendly because it breaks down faster in the atmosphere. These two refrigerants are not interchangeable -- using the wrong type can damage the system. Older vehicles made before 1994 used **R-12**, which is no longer produced due to its ozone-depleting properties. Always check the vehicle's underhood label to identify the correct refrigerant.

---

## Compressor Types and Function

The **compressor** is the heart of the A/C system. It is a belt-driven pump mounted on the engine that pressurizes refrigerant gas and pushes it through the system. Common types include piston compressors, rotary vane compressors, and scroll compressors. Many compressors use an electromagnetic **clutch** that engages and disengages the compressor as needed. Some newer vehicles use variable displacement compressors or electric compressors that can adjust their output without cycling on and off, providing smoother and more efficient cooling.

---

## Condenser Function

The **condenser** sits in front of the radiator, right behind the vehicle's grille, where it gets maximum airflow. Its job is to release heat from the refrigerant to the outside air. Hot, high-pressure refrigerant gas enters the condenser and, as air passes over its fins and tubes, the gas cools down and condenses into a high-pressure liquid. Think of it as the opposite of a radiator -- instead of cooling the engine, it cools the refrigerant. A blocked or damaged condenser will cause poor A/C performance and high system pressures.

---

## Evaporator Function

The **evaporator** is located inside the vehicle's dashboard, hidden behind the HVAC housing. Cold, low-pressure liquid refrigerant enters the evaporator and absorbs heat from the cabin air being blown across it by the blower motor. As the refrigerant absorbs heat, it boils and turns into a gas. The cabin air loses its heat and comes out cold through the dash vents. The evaporator also removes humidity from the air, which is why you see water dripping under your car on hot days -- that is condensation from moisture pulled out of the cabin air.

---

## Expansion Valve vs Orifice Tube

The **expansion device** is the component that drops the refrigerant's pressure, making it cold before it enters the evaporator. There are two common types. A **thermal expansion valve (TXV)** uses a sensing bulb to monitor evaporator temperature and adjusts refrigerant flow automatically for precise cooling. An **orifice tube** is a simpler, fixed-size restriction that allows a set amount of refrigerant to pass through. Systems using an orifice tube include an accumulator on the low side, while TXV systems use a receiver/drier on the high side. Both achieve the same goal but differ in design complexity.

---

## Receiver/Drier and Accumulator

These components remove moisture and filter debris from the refrigerant. A **receiver/drier** is used in TXV systems and is located on the high-pressure side between the condenser and the expansion valve. It stores extra liquid refrigerant and contains a bag of desiccant that absorbs moisture. An **accumulator** is used in orifice tube systems and sits on the low-pressure side between the evaporator and the compressor. It catches any liquid refrigerant that did not fully evaporate, preventing it from reaching and damaging the compressor. Both contain desiccant and should be replaced whenever the system is opened for major repairs.
