# A7 Module 3: Heating, Ventilation, and Controls

This module covers the heating and ventilation side of the HVAC system, along with the controls that let the driver adjust temperature, airflow direction, and fan speed. While the A/C side uses refrigerant to cool the cabin, the heating side uses engine coolant to warm it. Understanding how these systems work together is key to diagnosing comfort complaints from customers.

---

## Heater Core Function

The **heater core** is a small radiator-like component located inside the dashboard housing. Hot engine coolant flows through its tubes, and when the driver requests heat, the blower motor pushes cabin air across the heater core's fins. The air picks up heat from the coolant and enters the cabin as warm air. A leaking heater core can cause a sweet-smelling mist inside the vehicle, foggy windows, and coolant loss from the engine. Replacing a heater core is often a labor-intensive job because the entire dashboard may need to be partially or fully removed.

---

## Heater Control Valve

The **heater control valve** controls the flow of hot engine coolant into the heater core. In some vehicles, it is a vacuum-operated or cable-operated valve that opens and closes based on the temperature setting. When set to full cold, the valve closes and stops hot coolant from flowing through the heater core. Not all vehicles use a heater control valve -- many modern systems allow coolant to flow through the heater core at all times and use a blend door instead to control cabin temperature. When a heater control valve fails, you may get no heat or constant heat regardless of the setting.

---

## Blend Door and Mode Door

**Blend doors** and **mode doors** are flaps inside the HVAC housing that direct airflow. The **blend door** controls temperature by mixing hot air from the heater core with cool air from the evaporator. Moving the temperature slider adjusts how much of each you get. **Mode doors** control where the air comes out: through the dash vents, floor vents, or defrost vents. These doors are moved by small electric actuator motors or vacuum motors. A stuck or broken blend door actuator is a common cause of complaints like "heat only blows from the defrost vents" or "one side is hot and the other is cold."

---

## Blower Motor and Resistor

The **blower motor** is an electric fan that pushes air through the evaporator and heater core and out the cabin vents. It has multiple speed settings controlled by a **blower motor resistor** or, in newer vehicles, a transistor module. The resistor uses a series of wire coils that add resistance to reduce voltage and slow the fan. If the resistor fails, you may lose some or all fan speeds except the highest speed, which bypasses the resistor entirely. A blower motor that does not work at all could indicate a bad motor, a blown fuse, a faulty relay, or a wiring issue.

---

## Cabin Air Filter

The **cabin air filter** cleans the air before it enters the passenger compartment. It catches dust, pollen, leaves, and other debris, improving air quality for the people inside the vehicle. It is usually located behind the glove box or under the cowl area near the base of the windshield. A clogged cabin air filter restricts airflow, causing weak air from the vents, musty odors, and extra strain on the blower motor. Most manufacturers recommend replacing it every 15,000 to 30,000 miles, but this varies. It is one of the easiest maintenance items to check and replace.

---

## Automatic Temperature Control

Many modern vehicles feature **automatic temperature control (ATC)**, which lets the driver set a desired temperature and the system maintains it automatically. An ATC module receives input from sensors -- including an **in-car temperature sensor**, an **ambient (outside) temperature sensor**, and a **sunload sensor** -- and adjusts the blend door position, blower speed, and mode doors accordingly. If the cabin is too warm, the system increases A/C output and fan speed. If it is too cool, it blends in more heater air. Diagnosing ATC problems often involves using a scan tool to read sensor values and actuator commands.

---

## Engine Cooling System and Heater Circuit

The heating system is directly connected to the **engine cooling system**. Coolant heated by the engine is circulated by the water pump through the engine block, cylinder head, radiator, and heater core. A **thermostat** regulates engine temperature by controlling coolant flow to the radiator. The heater core is plumbed in parallel with the radiator, so some hot coolant always flows (or can flow) through it. If the engine is not reaching proper operating temperature due to a stuck-open thermostat, the heater will blow lukewarm air. Low coolant level can also cause poor heating because air pockets block flow through the heater core.

---

## HVAC Electrical Controls and Sensors

The HVAC system relies on a network of **electrical controls and sensors** to function properly. The **control head** (the panel on the dashboard) sends commands to actuators and relays. Key sensors include the **evaporator temperature sensor**, which prevents the evaporator from freezing, and the **coolant temperature sensor**, which helps the system decide when the engine is warm enough to provide heat. Pressure switches on the A/C lines protect the compressor by disabling it if pressure is too high or too low. Diagnosing electrical issues involves checking fuses, relays, ground connections, and using a scan tool to read sensor data and trouble codes.
