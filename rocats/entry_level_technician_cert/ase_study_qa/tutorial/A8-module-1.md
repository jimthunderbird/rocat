# A8 Module 1: Ignition and Fuel Systems

This module covers the two essential ingredients every engine needs to run: spark and fuel. You will learn how the ignition system creates and delivers a spark at precisely the right moment, how fuel is pressurized and sprayed into the engine, and how clean air enters the system. Understanding these basics is the first step toward diagnosing why an engine runs rough, misfires, or will not start at all.

---

## How Ignition Systems Work

The ignition system has one job: deliver a high-voltage spark to each cylinder at exactly the right time. It starts with the 12-volt battery and uses an ignition coil to boost that voltage to 20,000 to 50,000 volts. This high-voltage pulse travels to the spark plug, where it jumps a small gap and ignites the air-fuel mixture. Modern systems are controlled electronically by the engine computer, which determines precisely when each spark fires based on sensor inputs like engine speed, load, and temperature.

---

## Spark Plugs and Spark Plug Wires

A spark plug is a small but critical component that screws into the cylinder head. It has a center electrode and a ground electrode with a tiny gap between them. When high voltage arrives, a spark jumps across that gap. Over time, electrodes wear down and the gap widens, causing misfires. Spark plug wires carry the high voltage from the coil to the plug. Damaged or worn wires can leak voltage, causing weak sparks. Many newer vehicles have eliminated plug wires entirely by placing coils directly on top of each spark plug.

---

## Ignition Coils (COP, Waste Spark)

Ignition coils transform low battery voltage into the high voltage needed to fire a spark plug. There are several designs. In a **Coil-On-Plug (COP)** system, each cylinder gets its own individual coil mounted directly on the spark plug, eliminating the need for plug wires. A **waste spark** system pairs two cylinders together on one coil. The coil fires both plugs simultaneously -- one cylinder is on its compression stroke and actually uses the spark, while the other is on its exhaust stroke and "wastes" it. COP is the most common design in modern vehicles.

---

## Ignition Timing

Ignition timing refers to the exact moment the spark plug fires relative to the piston's position. If the spark fires too early (advanced timing), the fuel burns before the piston reaches the top, causing a knocking or pinging sound that can damage the engine. If the spark fires too late (retarded timing), power is lost and fuel economy drops. The engine computer continuously adjusts timing based on engine speed, load, temperature, and knock sensor input to find the ideal balance between power and efficiency for every driving condition.

---

## Fuel Injection Types (Port, Direct, TBI)

Modern engines use fuel injectors instead of carburetors. **Throttle Body Injection (TBI)** was an early system that sprayed fuel above the throttle body, similar to a carburetor. **Port Fuel Injection (PFI)** places an injector in each intake port, just before the intake valve, giving each cylinder its own precise fuel spray. **Gasoline Direct Injection (GDI)** sprays fuel directly into the combustion chamber at extremely high pressure, improving efficiency and power. Some newer engines combine port and direct injection to get the benefits of both systems.

---

## Fuel Pump and Fuel Pressure

The fuel pump delivers gasoline from the tank to the engine at a specific pressure. Most modern vehicles use an electric fuel pump located inside the fuel tank, submerged in gasoline which actually helps keep it cool. The pump typically produces 30 to 60 PSI for port injection systems and up to 2,000 PSI or more for direct injection systems. If the pump weakens, fuel pressure drops and the engine may hesitate, stall, or refuse to start. Technicians test fuel pressure with a gauge connected to the fuel rail to verify proper operation.

---

## Fuel Injector Cleaning and Testing

Fuel injectors are precision devices that spray a fine mist of fuel into the engine. Over time, deposits from gasoline can build up on the injector tips, disrupting the spray pattern and causing rough idle, misfires, or poor fuel economy. Technicians test injectors using a specialized tool called a noid light to check the electrical signal, or by measuring resistance with a multimeter. Cleaning can be done with a pressurized cleaning solution run through the fuel rail, or by removing the injectors and using an ultrasonic cleaner for severe cases.

---

## Fuel Pressure Regulator

The fuel pressure regulator maintains a consistent fuel pressure in the fuel rail regardless of engine load. In return-style systems, excess fuel is routed back to the tank through a return line. The regulator uses a spring-loaded diaphragm connected to intake manifold vacuum. Under high vacuum such as idle, fuel pressure is reduced slightly. Under heavy load when vacuum drops, pressure increases. Many modern vehicles use a returnless system where the regulator is built into the fuel pump module inside the tank and is controlled electronically by the engine computer.

---

## Air Filter and Intake System

The intake system delivers clean, measured air to the engine. It begins with an **air filter**, usually housed in a plastic airbox, which traps dust, dirt, and debris before they can enter the engine. A dirty air filter restricts airflow and reduces performance and fuel economy. After the filter, air flows through the **intake duct** past the mass airflow sensor, through the **throttle body** which controls how much air enters, and into the **intake manifold** which distributes air evenly to each cylinder. Keeping this entire path clean and sealed is essential for proper engine operation.
