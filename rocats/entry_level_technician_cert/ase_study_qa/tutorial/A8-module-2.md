# A8 Module 2: Emission Control Systems

This module covers the systems that reduce harmful pollutants in vehicle exhaust. Modern vehicles produce a fraction of the pollution that older cars did, thanks to a network of emission control devices monitored by the engine computer. You will learn what each system does, why it matters, and how technicians verify that everything is working correctly. Understanding emissions is essential for passing both the A8 exam and real-world smog inspections.

---

## Why Emission Controls Matter

Internal combustion engines produce three main pollutants: **hydrocarbons (HC)** from unburned fuel, **carbon monoxide (CO)** from incomplete combustion, and **oxides of nitrogen (NOx)** from extreme heat in the combustion chamber. These pollutants contribute to smog, respiratory illness, and environmental damage. Federal and state laws require vehicles to keep emissions below strict limits. Emission control systems work together to reduce these harmful byproducts while maintaining good engine performance. Technicians must understand these systems because a single failing component can cause a vehicle to fail an emissions test.

---

## Catalytic Converter Function

The **catalytic converter** is the most important emission control device on a vehicle. It is located in the exhaust system and uses precious metals like platinum, palladium, and rhodium as catalysts to trigger chemical reactions that clean the exhaust. A **three-way catalytic converter** performs three jobs: it converts carbon monoxide into carbon dioxide, converts hydrocarbons into water and carbon dioxide, and converts oxides of nitrogen into harmless nitrogen and oxygen. The converter must reach around 500 degrees Fahrenheit to work efficiently, which is why emissions are highest during cold starts before it warms up.

---

## Exhaust Gas Recirculation (EGR)

The **EGR system** reduces oxides of nitrogen (NOx) by recirculating a small portion of exhaust gas back into the intake manifold. Exhaust gas is inert, meaning it does not burn. When mixed with the incoming air-fuel charge, it lowers combustion temperatures. Since NOx forms at extremely high temperatures, this temperature reduction significantly cuts NOx emissions. The EGR valve opens and closes based on commands from the engine computer. A stuck-open EGR valve causes rough idle and stalling, while a stuck-closed valve causes detonation (engine knock) and increased NOx. Technicians test EGR operation with scan tools and vacuum gauges.

---

## Positive Crankcase Ventilation (PCV)

The **PCV system** is one of the oldest and simplest emission controls. During engine operation, some combustion gases leak past the piston rings into the crankcase, a process called blow-by. If left unchecked, these gases would build pressure, contaminate the oil, and release pollutants into the atmosphere. The PCV valve routes these blow-by gases from the crankcase back into the intake manifold where they are burned during normal combustion. The valve is spring-loaded and regulates flow based on engine vacuum. A clogged PCV valve can cause oil leaks, sludge buildup, and rough idle. Replacement is simple and inexpensive.

---

## Evaporative Emission Control (EVAP)

The **EVAP system** prevents gasoline vapors from escaping the fuel tank into the atmosphere. Fuel vapors are captured in a **charcoal canister** filled with activated charcoal that absorbs and stores them. When driving conditions are right, the engine computer opens a **purge valve** that allows fresh air to flow through the canister, carrying the stored vapors into the intake manifold to be burned. The system is sealed and must hold pressure. The computer periodically tests the system for leaks. A loose or missing gas cap is one of the most common causes of an EVAP trouble code and check engine light.

---

## Oxygen Sensors (O2 Sensors)

**Oxygen sensors** monitor the oxygen content in the exhaust gas, providing critical feedback to the engine computer for fuel control. The **upstream sensor** (before the catalytic converter) tells the computer whether the air-fuel mixture is running rich (too much fuel) or lean (too little fuel). The computer constantly adjusts fuel delivery based on this reading. The **downstream sensor** (after the converter) monitors catalytic converter efficiency. A healthy converter produces a steady downstream signal, while a failing converter produces a fluctuating signal similar to the upstream sensor. Most vehicles have two to four oxygen sensors depending on engine configuration.

---

## Secondary Air Injection

The **secondary air injection** system pumps fresh air into the exhaust stream, typically during cold starts. When the engine is cold, the air-fuel mixture runs rich to help the engine warm up smoothly, which produces extra hydrocarbons and carbon monoxide. The secondary air pump pushes ambient air into the exhaust manifold or directly into the catalytic converter to help burn off these excess pollutants. It also helps the catalytic converter reach its operating temperature faster. Once the engine warms up, the system typically shuts off. Not all vehicles have this system, but it is common on many modern engines.

---

## OBD-II Monitoring and Readiness

**OBD-II** (On-Board Diagnostics, second generation) is a standardized system required on all vehicles sold in the United States since 1996. It continuously monitors emission-related systems and stores trouble codes when problems are detected. The system runs a series of self-tests called **monitors**, including catalyst efficiency, EVAP system integrity, oxygen sensor function, EGR flow, and misfire detection. Each monitor has a **readiness status** that indicates whether the test has been completed. During an emissions inspection, all monitors must show "ready" or "complete." If a battery was recently disconnected or codes were cleared, monitors reset and need drive cycles to complete again.
