# G1 Module 2: Brakes, Steering, and Suspension Maintenance

This module covers the systems that keep a vehicle safe on the road. Brakes stop the vehicle, steering controls direction, and suspension keeps the tires in contact with the road surface. Proper maintenance of these systems is not just about comfort -- it is a matter of safety for the driver, passengers, and everyone else on the road. As a technician, you will inspect and service these components regularly, so understanding how they work and what to look for is essential.

---

## Brake Pad Replacement

Brake pads press against the brake rotor to create friction and slow the vehicle. They are the most commonly replaced brake component. Pads have a friction material layer that wears down with use. Most pads include a metal wear indicator tab that squeals when the pad is nearly worn out. To replace them, you remove the wheel, remove the caliper bolts, slide the caliper off the rotor, and remove the old pads. Before installing new pads, you push the caliper piston back into the caliper using a C-clamp or brake tool. Install the new pads, reinstall the caliper, and torque the bolts to spec. Always replace pads in pairs on the same axle.

---

## Brake Rotor Inspection

The **brake rotor** (also called a disc) is the flat, circular metal surface that the brake pads squeeze against. During inspection, you check for several conditions. **Minimum thickness** is stamped on the rotor -- if it has worn below this measurement, it must be replaced. **Lateral runout** is measured with a dial indicator and refers to wobble as the rotor spins; excessive runout causes a pulsation in the brake pedal. **Scoring** refers to grooves worn into the surface by worn pads. Light scoring can be corrected by resurfacing (machining) the rotor on a lathe, but if the rotor is too thin or warped, replacement is the safer and more common choice.

---

## Brake Fluid Flush

Brake fluid transmits hydraulic pressure from the brake pedal to the calipers and wheel cylinders. It is **hygroscopic**, meaning it absorbs moisture from the atmosphere over time. Moisture in brake fluid lowers its boiling point, which can cause **vapor lock** during heavy braking -- the fluid boils, creates gas bubbles, and the brake pedal goes soft or to the floor. A brake fluid flush replaces all the old fluid with fresh fluid. The process involves opening each bleeder valve one at a time, starting from the wheel farthest from the master cylinder, and pushing new fluid through until clean, clear fluid flows out with no air bubbles.

## Tire Rotation and Balancing

**Tire rotation** means moving tires to different positions on the vehicle to ensure they wear evenly. Front tires typically wear faster and on different edges than rear tires due to steering and weight distribution. Common rotation patterns include front-to-rear, cross patterns, or modified patterns for directional tires. Most manufacturers recommend rotating tires every 5,000 to 7,500 miles. **Tire balancing** uses a machine to identify heavy spots on a tire-and-wheel assembly and attaches small weights to counterbalance them. Unbalanced tires cause vibration at highway speeds and accelerate tire wear. Balancing should be done whenever tires are mounted or if the driver reports vibration.

---

## Tire Pressure Check and TPMS

Proper tire pressure is critical for safety, fuel economy, and tire life. Underinflated tires wear on the outer edges, overheat, and increase fuel consumption. Overinflated tires wear in the center and reduce traction. The correct pressure is listed on a sticker on the driver's door jamb, not on the tire sidewall (that number is the maximum). Use a quality tire pressure gauge to check pressure when tires are cold. The **Tire Pressure Monitoring System (TPMS)** uses sensors in each wheel to alert the driver when pressure drops below a threshold. There are two types: **direct TPMS** uses pressure sensors in each wheel, and **indirect TPMS** uses wheel speed sensors to detect differences in rotation caused by low pressure.

## Steering System Inspection

The steering system allows the driver to control the direction of the vehicle. Most modern vehicles use **rack and pinion** steering, while older trucks may use a **recirculating ball** system. During inspection, you check for play in the steering wheel with the engine running -- more than slight movement before the wheels respond indicates worn components. You inspect **tie rod ends** for looseness by grasping the tire at 3 and 9 o'clock and pushing in and out. Check **power steering fluid** level and condition, inspect hoses for leaks, and listen for whining from the power steering pump. Vehicles with electric power steering have no fluid but may have control module issues.

---

## Suspension Component Inspection

The suspension system absorbs road bumps and keeps the tires in contact with the road. Key components include **shock absorbers** and **struts**, which dampen bouncing. You test them by pushing down firmly on each corner of the vehicle and releasing -- the vehicle should bounce once and settle, not continue bouncing. **Ball joints** are pivot points that allow the wheel to move up and down while steering left and right. You check them by jacking up the wheel and checking for play. **Control arm bushings** are rubber cushions that absorb vibration. Cracked or deteriorated bushings cause clunking noises and wandering. **Springs** (coil or leaf) support the vehicle's weight and should be inspected for cracks or sagging.

---

## Wheel Bearing Service

**Wheel bearings** allow the wheel hub to spin freely with minimal friction. They support the vehicle's weight and endure tremendous forces during cornering and braking. A failing wheel bearing typically produces a humming or growling noise that changes with vehicle speed and may get louder when turning. To diagnose, raise the vehicle and spin each wheel by hand, feeling for roughness or play. You can also grasp the tire at 12 and 6 o'clock and push in and out to check for looseness. Most modern vehicles use sealed, pre-packed hub bearing assemblies that are replaced as a unit when they fail, rather than being repacked with grease like older style tapered roller bearings.
