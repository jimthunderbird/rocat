# G1 Module 3: Electrical and HVAC Maintenance

This module covers basic electrical system maintenance and heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) tasks that entry-level technicians perform regularly. The electrical system powers everything from the engine starter to the headlights, while the HVAC system keeps the cabin comfortable in all weather. You do not need to be an electrical engineer to handle these tasks -- with a basic understanding of circuits and the right tools, you can confidently diagnose and repair common electrical and climate control issues.

---

## Battery Testing and Replacement

The battery stores electrical energy and provides the power needed to start the engine. Over time, batteries lose their ability to hold a charge. To test a battery, technicians use a **digital battery tester** or a **carbon pile load tester** that puts the battery under a heavy electrical load and measures how well it responds. Before testing, check the battery terminals for corrosion -- a white or greenish powdery buildup that can prevent good electrical contact. Clean the terminals with a battery terminal brush and a mixture of baking soda and water. When replacing a battery, always disconnect the negative cable first and reconnect it last to prevent sparks. Note that some vehicles may need their radio or computer settings reprogrammed after battery replacement.

## Headlight Bulb Replacement

Headlight bulbs eventually burn out and need replacement. Most vehicles use one of three types: **halogen** bulbs are the most common and affordable, **HID (xenon)** bulbs produce a brighter, bluish-white light, and **LED** headlights are becoming increasingly standard on newer vehicles. For halogen bulbs, replacement usually involves accessing the back of the headlight assembly from the engine compartment, twisting out the bulb socket, and installing the new bulb without touching the glass with bare fingers, as skin oils create hot spots that shorten bulb life. HID and LED systems may require additional components or calibration. Always replace bulbs in pairs so both headlights have matching brightness and color.

## Fuse Diagnosis and Replacement

**Fuses** protect electrical circuits from damage caused by excessive current. When too much current flows through a circuit, the thin metal strip inside the fuse melts and breaks the circuit, preventing wires from overheating or catching fire. To diagnose a blown fuse, locate the fuse box (usually under the dashboard or under the hood), use the diagram on the fuse box cover to find the correct fuse, and pull it out with a fuse puller. Visually inspect the metal strip -- if it is broken or blackened, the fuse is blown. Replace it with a fuse of the exact same amperage rating. If the new fuse blows immediately, there is a short circuit in that circuit that needs further diagnosis.

## Wiper Blade Replacement

Wiper blades clear rain, snow, and debris from the windshield. They are made of rubber that degrades from sun exposure, temperature changes, and normal use, typically lasting six months to a year. Signs of worn blades include streaking, smearing, chattering (a skipping or vibrating motion), or missed areas. Replacement is straightforward: lift the wiper arm away from the windshield, press the release tab on the blade adapter, slide the old blade off, and click the new one into place. Be careful not to let the bare wiper arm snap back against the windshield, as it can crack the glass. Always check that the new blades are the correct length for the vehicle.

## Cabin Air Filter Replacement

The **cabin air filter** cleans the air that enters the passenger compartment through the HVAC system. It traps dust, pollen, mold spores, and other airborne particles, keeping the air inside the vehicle clean and improving comfort for passengers with allergies. Most cabin air filters are located behind the glove box or under the dashboard. To replace it, you usually remove or lower the glove box, open the filter housing, slide out the old filter, and insert the new one paying attention to the airflow direction arrow printed on the filter. A clogged cabin air filter reduces airflow from the vents and can cause a musty odor. Replacement is typically recommended every 15,000 to 20,000 miles.

## A/C System Inspection

The air conditioning system removes heat and humidity from the cabin air. It works by compressing and expanding a **refrigerant** (usually R-134a or R-1234yf) through a closed loop of components. During a basic inspection, technicians check the **drive belt** that powers the compressor, listen for the compressor clutch engaging when A/C is turned on, feel the temperature of the lines (the high side should be warm and the low side should be cold), and check for visible oil stains that indicate refrigerant leaks. Refrigerant pressures are measured with a manifold gauge set connected to the service ports. Note that refrigerant must be handled carefully because releasing it into the atmosphere is illegal and requires EPA Section 608 or 609 certification to service.

## Charging System Check

The **charging system** keeps the battery charged and powers all electrical systems while the engine is running. It consists of the **alternator**, the **voltage regulator** (often built into the alternator), and the connecting wiring. To test the charging system, use a multimeter across the battery terminals with the engine running. A healthy alternator should produce between 13.5 and 14.8 volts. Below that range, the battery is not being adequately charged. You can also use a scan tool or dedicated charging system tester for more detailed analysis. Common symptoms of a failing alternator include a dim or flickering dashboard light, a dead battery, or a whining noise from the alternator. Check the drive belt and connections before condemning the alternator.

## Basic Electrical Testing with Multimeter

A **multimeter** is an essential tool for any technician. It measures **voltage** (electrical pressure), **resistance** (opposition to current flow), and **current** (flow of electricity). To measure **voltage**, set the meter to DC volts, connect the red lead to the positive point and the black lead to ground, and read the display. To measure **resistance**, disconnect the component from the circuit first, set the meter to ohms, and place the leads across the component. To check for **continuity**, use the continuity or ohms setting to verify a wire or switch provides a complete path for electricity. A reading of **OL** (over limit) means an open circuit with no path for current. Learning to use a multimeter confidently is one of the most valuable skills for an entry-level technician.
