Summary

The Antikythera mechanism is an ancient Greek hand-powered orrery and the oldest known example of an analogue computer, dating to approximately 150–100 BCE. Recovered in 1901 from a Roman-era shipwreck off the Greek island of Antikythera by sponge divers working with the Hellenic Royal Navy, the device remained unrecognised for two years until archaeologist Valerios Stais spotted a gear wheel embedded in one of the corroded bronze lumps. Housed in a wooden case roughly the size of a shoebox (34 × 18 × 9 cm), the mechanism contained at least 37 interlocking bronze gears capable of predicting the positions of the Sun and Moon against the zodiac, modelling the Moon’s irregular orbit through a sophisticated pin-and-slot device, forecasting solar and lunar eclipses via the 223-month Saros cycle, and tracking the four-year cycle of Panhellenic athletic games including the ancient Olympics.

Its front dial displayed the zodiac and an Egyptian calendar ring, while the rear carried two large spiral dials—the Metonic dial (235 months over 19 years) for calendar alignment and the Saros dial for eclipse prediction—each with subsidiary dials for finer corrections. Inscriptions deciphered through X-ray tomography in 2005 by the Antikythera Mechanism Research Project revealed synodic cycle data for Venus (462) and Saturn (442), confirming the device’s extraordinary scope. The astronomical theories it encodes, particularly Hipparchus of Rhodes’ lunar model and Babylonian arithmetic prediction methods, link it to the vibrant scientific tradition of Hellenistic Rhodes.

No comparable technological complexity appeared again until the astronomical clocks of 14th-century Europe, though partial transmission occurred through Byzantine geared calendars and Islamic mechanical traditions. The mechanism thus stands as powerful evidence that the ancient Greeks possessed engineering and mathematical capabilities far beyond what was previously believed, representing a lost tradition of precision instrument-making that would not be rediscovered for over a thousand years. Today all 82 surviving fragments reside in the National Archaeological Museum of Athens.
